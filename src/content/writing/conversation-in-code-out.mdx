In September I started building a product with people who don't write code. My business co-founder knows the market, our legal advisor knows what we can promise a technician, and a few weeks in, a marketing lead joined. Lots of judgment, very little shared vocabulary.

The product is Project Handyman (working name), a home-repair marketplace for Mexico. The hard side is supply. Most plumbers, electricians and HVAC technicians here work informally and find jobs by word of mouth, so one month is packed and the next is empty. We want the work to come to them, along with a verified profile and a reputation that grows over time. That raises questions about labor law, payments and trust long before it raises questions about code.

Teams like this usually end up with one person who sits in every meeting and turns it into tickets and specs. That person was going to be me, and it stops working after a few weeks. So before writing much code, I designed how we would work.

## Three rules

**People talk.** Our best thinking happens on long calls where we walk through the product screen by screen. Every call is recorded and processed, and nobody types into Jira by hand. Talking is the interface.

**Standard tools.** Jira, Confluence, Figma, GitHub and Loom. A new engineer, advisor or investor can find their way around on day one, and nothing important depends on me remembering it.

**Claude connects everything.** Through MCP, the same agent can read a transcript, update a ticket, write a Confluence page and check a Figma frame. That's how a decision made by someone who doesn't code reaches engineering without me relaying it.

## The layer in between

Most of my October went into the layer between product and engineering: deciding where each kind of information lives, writing the conventions that make a ticket a decision, and tuning the routines until an hour-long call could turn into tickets with nobody taking notes.

A conversation goes through six steps before it becomes code:

<Workflow>
  <Step n="1" title="Conversation" tool="Loom">The call is recorded.</Step>
  <Step n="2" title="Triage" tool="Claude + MCP">A routine reads the transcript, writes a summary and action items, and updates or opens tickets tagged with the meeting date.</Step>
  <Step n="3" title="Decision" tool="Jira">Each open question is a ticket. Its owner settles it on a call or by telling Claude, which records the outcome and closes it.</Step>
  <Step n="4" title="Record" tool="Confluence">An agent adds the outcome to a dated decision log. Technical choices also get an ADR.</Step>
  <Step n="5" title="Design" tool="Figma">If a decision changes a screen, it opens a design task. Each frame lists the tickets behind it.</Step>
  <Step n="6" title="Build" tool="GitHub">Engineers and coding agents get tickets with the decision and the design already linked.</Step>
</Workflow>

Jira tickets are disposable. Once the outcome lands in Confluence, Figma or the code, the ticket is done, and nobody has to dig through old ones to learn how the product works. The reference side has one rule: every piece of information lives in exactly one place. When our product docs existed in both the repo and Confluence, I deleted the thousand lines in the repo and left a README pointing to Confluence.

### Agents write, people decide

The meeting routine runs almost unsupervised, and I'm fine with that. A slightly wrong ticket takes a minute to fix. A conversation nobody wrote down is gone for good.

Documentation is the opposite. Agents write every Confluence page and architecture record, but only after long working sessions where I argue the problem through and make each call. My partners work the same way with Jira: they ask Claude what's on their plate, tell it what they decided, and Claude updates the tickets.

## Working with people who don't write code

**Walkthroughs over specs.** We review the prototype screen by screen, so feedback lands on something concrete. On one call someone mentioned, almost as an aside, that you couldn't pick a technician on the offers screen. Nobody wrote it down. It still ended up as a bug ticket tagged with that meeting, headed for Figma and the code.

**Costs in pesos.** Every technical option comes with a monthly price. Once we priced a text message for every sign-in, nobody needed a technical argument to drop one-time codes for passwords.

<Callout>
When a decision changes, the old version stays in the log, crossed out, with the date and the reason. It keeps a small team from reopening the same argument every other meeting.
</Callout>

- **A designed product.** Customer and technician flows in Figma.
- **A working prototype.** Our first screens came out of an AI design tool as a 3,000-line web app. I used it as a reference and rebuilt it as a native Expo app for both sides of the marketplace, with an `AGENTS.md` that tells coding agents how the project works.
- **An approved architecture.** One Expo app, three NestJS services on Cloud Run, Firestore and Cloud Storage behind the API, and domain events on Pub/Sub. Each choice has an ADR with the alternatives we rejected and what it costs.
- **A team building from tickets.** Another engineer now picks up work with the decisions and designs already attached.

## What's still rough

- **Everything goes through me.** Fast at the start, a bottleneck now. Plenty of legal and financial questions still have no owner.
- **Alignment is manual.** Tickets, docs, designs and the prototype drift apart between meetings, and we fix it when someone notices. That needs a real process.
- **Design is ahead of code.** The latest decisions are in Figma; the prototype hasn't caught up.
- **Code hygiene came last.** Early commits went straight to main with no pull requests or CI. Fine solo, a problem with a team.

## What's next

Owners for every ticket, CI and app builds so the whole team can install the prototype, the first backend services, and the legal and tax questions between us and a pilot. Later I want the same discipline on the code side, with a spec, research, plan, execute and verify pipeline for coding agents that I'm building as an [open framework](https://github.com/nthchild2/agentic-software-development-framework).
