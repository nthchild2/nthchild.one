On October 6, while comparing our business model with our prototype, we found that they disagreed about how the platform's commission was calculated. The model applied it to labor only; the prototype applied it to the whole price. Nobody had decided which was right, so the question became a ticket.

Three weeks later it had been decided, logged and turned into work for design and engineering, and nobody had to remember it along the way:

That is the workflow this post is about. It's how we're building Project Handyman (working name), the first product of Obelisk Software, the studio I co-founded this year: a two-sided marketplace where people in Mexico describe a home repair and certified plumbers, electricians and HVAC technicians nearby send offers.

## Before launch, the work is mostly decisions

At this stage there is little code to write and a lot to decide. Is a technician a contractor or an employee? How long do we hold a payment before releasing it? Can customers pay in cash? Can one person be both a customer and a technician? Each answer shapes the screens, the data model and the contracts, and many of them depend on lawyers and accountants rather than engineers.

The people deciding are a mix: two co-founders, only one of them technical, a legal advisor, a marketing lead and an engineer. Most of the thinking happens out loud, in long calls. Left alone, that produces what it always produces: decisions scattered across recordings, chat threads and documents that slowly stop agreeing with each other, and the same questions argued again a few weeks later.

So I stopped treating code as the unit of work and started treating decisions that way. A decision gets what a code change gets: a place to live, a status, an owner, and a history anyone can read later.

## The pipeline

<Workflow>
  <Step n="1" title="Conversation" tool="Recording">Calls are recorded. The recording is raw material, not the record.</Step>
  <Step n="2" title="Triage" tool="Claude + Loom">A Claude routine pulls the transcript through MCP, writes a summary and action items, and updates or opens tickets tagged with the meeting they came from.</Step>
  <Step n="3" title="Decision" tool="Jira">Every open question is a ticket. Whoever owns it writes the outcome there and closes it.</Step>
  <Step n="4" title="Record" tool="Confluence">An agent writes the outcome into a dated decision log, linked to its ticket. Architecture decisions also get an ADR.</Step>
  <Step n="5" title="Design" tool="Figma">If a decision changes a screen, it opens a design task, and each frame lists the tickets that shaped it.</Step>
  <Step n="6" title="Build" tool="Repo">Implementation tickets reach engineers or coding agents with the decision and the design already linked.</Step>
</Workflow>

None of these steps needs a meeting about the process. The only thing people have to do differently is write the decision where it belongs.

## The rules that make it work

### Every kind of information has exactly one home

Jira holds what changes: open questions, decisions in progress, work to do. Confluence holds what people need to read: the product vision, the decision log, the business model, the architecture. Figma holds the screens and the repository holds the code. Confluence pages pull open items live from Jira instead of copying them, so a list is never stale.

The rule cost something. Early on, our product documents lived both in the repository and in Confluence. Rather than keep two copies in sync, I deleted the ones in the repository, about a thousand lines, and left a README that points to Confluence.

### Decisions are versioned, not overwritten

Decisions change, and in five weeks many of ours did. We went from one account that could switch between customer and technician to two separate accounts. We replaced one-time codes with passwords for signing in. We restructured how the commission is split and shortened the window for filing a claim. Each time, the old decision stays in the log, struck through, with the date and the reason it was replaced.

<Callout>
When someone asks why something works the way it does, the answer includes what we tried first. That history is what stops a small team from reopening the same question every other meeting.
</Callout>

### Agents write, people decide

This kind of process usually dies because keeping it up by hand is tedious. So agents do the writing and people do the deciding. All of it runs through Claude, connected to Loom, Jira, Confluence, Figma and GitHub through MCP servers.

Meeting triage is the most automated part. A routine pulls each transcript from Loom, writes the summary and action items, and updates or opens tickets on its own. I barely supervise it, and that's a deliberate trade: a ticket that's slightly off is cheap to fix, and a conversation nobody wrote down is gone.

Documentation works the other way around. I don't write the Confluence pages or the architecture records, but nothing reaches them without a long working session first: I argue through the problem with the agent, make each call, and the agent turns the result into the page. The prose is the agent's; the decisions in it are ours.

My business co-founder never needs to learn Jira's query language: a decision written in plain Spanish in the ticket is enough, and the rest of the chain follows from it.

### Technical decisions get the same treatment

Architecture questions are tickets too, and each one closes with an Architecture Decision Record: what we chose, which alternatives we rejected and why, and what it costs. Over two days in October I worked through eleven of them with an agent, covering the mobile client, backend services, data, real-time events, notifications, identity, AI, payments, hosting and observability. The short version:

- **One Expo app** with separate customer and technician experiences, instead of a web app.
- **Three NestJS services on Cloud Run**, split by security boundary and how they run rather than one service per entity.
- **Firestore and Cloud Storage behind the API**, never accessed directly from the phone.
- **Domain events on Pub/Sub from day one**, so real-time features can be added later without rewiring.

Cost was a hard constraint, and it settled several debates quickly. Services scale to zero. The diagnostic assistant runs on a free AI tier in development, behind a provider that can be swapped through configuration. And we dropped one-time login codes once we priced a text message for every sign-in and every switch between a customer and a technician account. Putting a monthly cost next to each option let the non-technical side of the team weigh technical trade-offs in business terms.

## A prototype built to be read by agents

Our first screens came out of an AI design tool as a single 3,000-line web app. It was useful for agreeing on what we were building and useless as the base for a native app, so I used it as a reference and rebuilt it in Expo and Expo Router, covering both the customer and the technician flows.

The structure is simple on purpose: thin route files, one folder per screen with the components only that screen uses, a shared folder for the rest, and an `AGENTS.md` file that tells coding agents how the project is organized, which SDK documentation to trust, and which checks to run before calling something done. Every decision that reaches the code arrives as a ticket with its context attached, which is exactly what an agent, or a new engineer, needs to start.

## What hasn't worked yet

- **Everything goes through me.** Almost every ticket and every architecture record passed through my hands. That was fast at the start and is a bottleneck now. Many legal and financial tickets still have no owner.
- **Alignment happens on demand.** Tickets, Confluence, Figma and the prototype drift apart between meetings, and today we realign them when someone notices. That works at our size, but it needs a structured process that keeps everything current without anyone having to remember to ask.
- **Design runs ahead of code.** The latest decisions are in Figma, but the prototype hasn't caught up. That's fine for a few days and risky for a few weeks.
- **The meeting log fell behind.** The first meetings are summarized in detail; later ones only show up through the tickets and decisions they produced.
- **Code hygiene came last.** Early commits went straight to the main branch with no pull requests or CI. That was acceptable with one developer and isn't with a team.

## What's next

Next is giving every ticket an owner, getting app builds and CI running so the whole team can install the prototype, standing up the first backend services, and closing the legal and tax questions that stand between us and a pilot. The next parts of this series will cover how we design that alignment process, how the backend is taking shape, and how we split implementation work between engineers and coding agents.
