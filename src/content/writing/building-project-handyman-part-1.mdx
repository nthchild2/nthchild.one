Project Handyman (working name) is the first product of Obelisk Software, the studio I co-founded this year. It's a two-sided marketplace for home services in Mexico: a customer describes a problem with text, photos or a voice note, gets a preliminary estimate, and certified plumbers, electricians and HVAC technicians nearby send offers. Instead of the customer chasing providers, the job finds the technician.

We're early. There's no backend in production and no customers yet. This series is a record of how we're building it, starting with the part I think matters most at this stage: how a team of founders, advisors and engineers makes and keeps track of decisions.

## The starting point

The first five weeks looked like most early-stage projects:

- **Long, dense meetings.** Five sessions in two weeks covered the customer flow, the technician flow, how a service actually runs, and a walkthrough with our legal advisor. Each one produced a dozen decisions and twice as many open questions.
- **A mix of people.** A business co-founder, a legal advisor, then a marketing lead and a backend engineer. Not everyone reads code, and not everyone was in every meeting.
- **Questions that weren't technical.** Is a technician a contractor or an employee? How long do we hold a payment before releasing it? What happens when a job is never closed? These block product decisions, and they move at the speed of lawyers and accountants, not sprints.
- **A prototype nobody could build on.** Our first screens came out of an AI design tool as a single 3,000-line web app. It was great for talking about the product and useless as a foundation for a native mobile app.

Decisions were scattered across recordings, chat threads and Markdown files in the repo, and nobody could say with confidence which version was current.

## The workflow

I set up a pipeline where every piece of work has exactly one home and moves in one direction:

<Workflow>
  <Step n="1" title="Meeting">Recorded and transcribed. Nothing said in a meeting is lost or depends on someone's notes.</Step>
  <Step n="2" title="Tickets">An AI agent drafts Jira tickets from the transcript, labelled with the meeting date, for me to review and file under the right epic.</Step>
  <Step n="3" title="Decision">Every open question is a ticket. When it's settled, the outcome is written as a "DECISION:" comment and the ticket closes.</Step>
  <Step n="4" title="Decision log">Confluence keeps a dated log of every decision and its source. Open decisions are pulled live from Jira, never copied.</Step>
  <Step n="5" title="Design">Figma frames link to the tickets that changed them, and each ticket links back to its frame.</Step>
  <Step n="6" title="Code">Implementation tickets go to engineers, and the repo carries instructions for AI coding agents.</Step>
</Workflow>

Three rules hold it together.

### One source of truth per kind of information

Jira holds what changes often: open questions, decisions in progress, work to do. Confluence holds what should be read: the vision, the decision log, the business model and the architecture. Figma holds the screens. The code repo holds code.

For a while the product docs lived in both the repo and Confluence. Rather than keep two copies in sync, I deleted the ones in the repo, about a thousand lines, and left a README that points to Confluence.

### Decisions are versioned, not overwritten

Decisions change, and they changed a lot. In five weeks we reversed how customer and technician accounts relate to each other, changed how users sign in, restructured the commission, and shortened the claims window. Each time, the old decision stays in the log, struck through, with the date and the reason it was replaced.

<Callout>
When someone asks "why does it work this way?", the answer includes what we tried first. That history is what keeps a small team from relitigating the same question every other meeting.
</Callout>

### Architecture decisions get their own records

Technical choices are tickets too, and each one closes with an Architecture Decision Record: the decision, the alternatives we rejected and why, and what it costs. On the 12th and 13th of October I wrote eleven of them (the payments one is still a proposal, waiting on a business decision):

- **Mobile client:** one Expo app with separate customer and technician experiences, instead of a PWA.
- **Backend:** three NestJS services on Cloud Run, split by security boundary and execution model rather than one microservice per entity.
- **Data:** Firestore and Cloud Storage behind the API, never accessed directly from the app.
- **Real-time:** push notifications and refresh for the pilot, with every service publishing domain events to Pub/Sub so we can add WebSockets later without rewiring.
- **Notifications, identity, AI, payments, hosting, observability:** each with its own record and cost estimate.

## Cost as a design constraint

At this stage we want running costs as close to zero as possible, so I treated cost as a hard constraint. That settled several debates quickly:

- **Scale-to-zero services.** Nothing runs when nobody is using it.
- **Free-tier AI in development.** The diagnostic assistant uses a provider that can be swapped through configuration, so we start on a free tier with fake data and move to a production provider when real users arrive.
- **Password sign-in instead of one-time codes.** We first planned to sign users in with a code sent by WhatsApp or SMS. Once we priced a message per login, and per switch between a customer and a technician account, we moved to email and password and verify the phone number once at sign-up.

Next to each decision, the infrastructure and per-service operating cost estimates helped the non-technical side of the team weigh in on technical trade-offs in their own terms.

## Rebuilding the prototype

The AI-generated mockup did its job: it got everyone talking about the same screens. But it was a web app, and the product is a native mobile app. Instead of adapting it, I used it as a reference and rebuilt it as an Expo / Expo Router app covering both the customer and the technician flows.

The new structure is simple on purpose: thin route files, one folder per screen with the components only that screen uses, a shared folder for the rest, and an `AGENTS.md` file that tells AI coding agents how the project works, which SDK version to check docs for, and which commands to run before calling something done. It works for the next engineer too.

## What hasn't worked (yet)

- **Everything goes through me.** I wrote nearly every ticket and every architecture record. That was fast at the start; now it's a bottleneck. Many of the legal and business tickets still have no owner.
- **Design runs ahead of code.** The latest decisions are in Figma, but the prototype hasn't caught up. That's fine for a few days and dangerous for a few weeks.
- **The meeting log fell behind.** The first five meetings are summarized in detail. The later ones only show up through the tickets and decisions they produced.
- **Code hygiene came last.** Early commits went straight to the main branch without pull requests or CI. That was acceptable alone and isn't with a team.

## What's next

The next steps are giving every ticket an owner, getting app builds and CI running so the team can install the prototype on their phones, standing up the first backend services, and starting to close the legal and tax questions that gate a pilot. The next part of this series will cover how the backend took shape and how we split work between people and AI agents.
